\begin{abstract}
The Interval Job Shop Scheduling Problem (IJSP) models uncertainty in task
durations by closed intervals and asks for operation orderings that are
robust under interval arithmetic. The state of the art is dominated by
per-instance metaheuristic search. We present, to the best of our
knowledge, the first learned \emph{constructive} policy for the IJSP: a
deep reinforcement learning (DRL) agent that dispatches operations one at a
time and whose policy and value networks are, by construction,
\emph{independent of the instance size}. Size invariance is obtained by a
dimensionless, uncertainty-aware feature design---all temporal quantities
are normalized by an instance lower bound and interval widths enter as
relative magnitudes---combined with a Deep Sets encoder over the set of
eligible operations and a pooled global context. Trained with proximal
policy optimization on only four $20{\times}15$ Taillard IJSP instances,
the policy attains a mean relative error of $13.4\%$ ($12.3\%$ best of
three seeds) against crisp Taillard lower bounds on unseen $20{\times}15$
instances, versus ${\approx}46\%$ for the best priority dispatching rule,
constructing solutions in milliseconds. The same single network, evaluated
zero-shot, outperforms the best dispatching rule on every tested instance
of all seven Taillard size classes ($15{\times}15$ to $50{\times}20$).
A pre-registered, anchor-verified experimental methodology supports three
further findings of practical relevance: sampling-based best-of-$N$
inference yields consistent gains; self-attention over eligible operations
does not improve on pooling at practical training budgets; and a
single-size specialist generalizes across sizes better than multi-size
training, even at an equalized per-instance budget. We additionally
characterize the policy as an initializer for state-of-the-art tabu
search populations, whose median sample dominates the best solution of
randomized dispatching generators, and lay out a pre-registered
hybridization study.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} job shop scheduling; interval uncertainty; deep
reinforcement learning; neural combinatorial optimization; size
invariance; hybrid metaheuristics.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Job Shop Scheduling Problem (JSP) is among the most studied NP-hard
combinatorial optimization problems~\cite{GareyJohnson1976}, both for its
practical relevance in manufacturing and for its notorious
difficulty---small instances proposed in the 1960s remained open for
decades. In real production environments, the durations of operations are
rarely known with precision at the time scheduling decisions must be made:
tool wear, human factors, and material variability all introduce
uncertainty. Among the frameworks proposed to capture this
uncertainty---stochastic, fuzzy~\cite{GonzalezRodriguez2008} and
robust formulations---the \emph{interval} representation stands out for
requiring the least information from the decision maker: each duration is
only assumed to lie within a closed interval, with no distributional or
membership assumptions~\cite{Lei2012,DiazICAE2023}. The resulting Interval
JSP (IJSP) evaluates a schedule with interval arithmetic, so its makespan
is itself an interval, and solutions are compared through a ranking
criterion such as the worst case or the expected (midpoint)
makespan~\cite{DiazICAE2023}.

The current state of the art for makespan minimization in the IJSP is
per-instance metaheuristic search: artificial bee colony algorithms
\cite{DiazFEABC2023,DiazICAE2023} and irace-tuned tabu search with
specialized neighbourhoods~\cite{DiazTS2026} obtain mean relative errors
(RE, against the crisp Taillard lower bounds) between roughly $0\%$ and
$14\%$ depending on the size class, at a computational cost of seconds to
minutes of search \emph{per instance and per run}.

A complementary paradigm has emerged in the deterministic scheduling
literature: \emph{learning to dispatch}, where a deep reinforcement
learning (DRL) agent constructs a schedule in a single pass by repeatedly
choosing the next operation among the eligible ones
\cite{Zhang2020L2D,Park2021GNN,Tassel2021}. Once trained, such a policy
amortizes its cost over arbitrarily many instances and produces solutions
in milliseconds, making it attractive (a) as a fast standalone solver in
time-critical or embedded settings, and (b) as a high-quality initializer
for search-based methods. To our knowledge, no learned constructive policy
had been proposed or evaluated for the interval variant of the JSP.

Bringing DRL to the IJSP raises two specific challenges. First,
\emph{uncertainty must enter the state representation}: the agent should
distinguish an operation with duration $[10,11]$ from one with $[5,16]$,
since a min--max (or expected-value) objective values them very
differently. Second, and more structurally, standard JSP-DRL
architectures tie network dimensions to the instance size (e.g.\ value
functions over fixed-size state vectors), so a policy trained on one size
class cannot even be \emph{evaluated} on another. This is at odds with the
practical promise of learned solvers, which is precisely to amortize
training across a distribution of instances.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This work makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A size-invariant, uncertainty-aware DRL policy for the
  IJSP} (Section~\ref{sec:method}). Every feature is dimensionless:
  temporal quantities are normalized by a per-instance lower bound, and
  interval uncertainty enters as relative widths. A Deep
  Sets~\cite{Zaheer2017} encoder over the eligible-operation set, combined
  with a pooled global context, yields policy \emph{and} value networks
  with ${\approx}1.2\times10^{5}$ parameters independent of the numbers of
  jobs and machines.
\item \textbf{A rigorous, pre-registered experimental methodology}
  (Section~\ref{sec:setup}): a two-stage acceptance protocol (cheap filter
  benchmark, full confirmation benchmark) with paired seeds,
  bit-reproducible runs, and \emph{deterministic anchors}---dispatching
  rules whose results must remain identical across all code versions,
  ensuring that measured improvements are attributable to learning and
  not to inadvertent environment changes.
\item \textbf{An empirical characterization} (Section~\ref{sec:results}):
  training-budget scaling on the training size class; zero-shot transfer
  that dominates the best dispatching rule on all seven Taillard size
  classes from a single network; and three controlled ablations
  (best-of-$N$ inference, attention versus pooling, specialist versus
  multi-size training), two of which are cautionary negative results for
  the field.
\item \textbf{A bridge to hybrid search} (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}): a
  characterization of the policy as a generator of initial populations
  for the tabu search of~\cite{DiazTS2026}, together with a
  pre-registered pilot design measuring anytime convergence under
  different seeding fractions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Job shop scheduling under interval uncertainty}

Scheduling under uncertainty has been addressed through stochastic
programming, fuzzy sets and interval analysis. Fuzzy processing times,
usually triangular, have received sustained attention in the JSP
\cite{GonzalezRodriguez2008,Palacios2015}; the interval representation can
be seen as its least-committal special case, requiring only support
bounds. Lei~\cite{Lei2012} introduced population-based neighbourhood
search for the JSP with interval processing times; subsequent work
developed dedicated interval ranking operators and robustness analyses.
The recent IJSP literature centred on the Taillard-derived benchmark
\cite{DiazFEABC2023,DiazICAE2023,DiazTS2026} establishes artificial bee
colony variants and tabu search as the strongest published solvers, and
fixes the evaluation conventions adopted here (interval arithmetic,
lexicographic ranking during search, expected makespan for reporting).

\subsection{Neural combinatorial optimization and DRL for scheduling}

Neural approaches to combinatorial optimization began with pointer
networks~\cite{Vinyals2015} and their RL training~\cite{Bello2016}, with
attention-based encoders becoming standard for routing
\cite{Kool2019,Nazari2018}. For the deterministic JSP,
Zhang et al.~\cite{Zhang2020L2D} learn dispatching policies over
disjunctive-graph GNN embeddings; Park et al.~\cite{Park2021GNN} refine
GNN representations and report generalization across sizes; Tassel et
al.~\cite{Tassel2021} provide an efficient RL environment formulation.
Sampling-based decoding (best-of-$N$) is a standard inference-time
improvement~\cite{Kool2019}. Permutation-invariant set encoders were
formalized as Deep Sets~\cite{Zaheer2017}; attention is their natural
strict generalization. Our architecture deliberately starts from the
simpler Deep Sets construction and evaluates attention as a controlled
ablation---a comparison that, at our budgets, favours the simpler model.
An older, complementary line of \emph{learned} constructive heuristics is
the automated design of dispatching rules by genetic programming
(GP)~\cite{Branke2016,Nguyen2017}, which evolves interpretable priority
expressions over hand-crafted attributes. We include a GP hyper-heuristic
trained on the same instances as our policy as a learned symbolic
baseline, quantifying what the neural representation adds over the best
evolvable closed-form rule.

\subsection{Hybrid learning and search}

Combining learned constructive policies with local search follows the
long-standing observation that metaheuristic performance depends on
initialization quality and diversity. GRASP formalizes randomized greedy
construction followed by local search~\cite{Feo1995}; in neural
combinatorial optimization, learned constructions have been combined with
beam search, sampling and improvement operators~\cite{Kool2019,
Zhang2020L2D}. We contribute an initialization study specific to the
IJSP, with the population-based tabu search of~\cite{DiazTS2026} as the
host solver and randomized dispatching as the fair baseline generator.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

\begin{definition}[IJSP]
An IJSP instance comprises $n$ jobs $J_1,\dots,J_n$ and $m$ machines. Job
$J_j$ is a fixed sequence of $m$ operations $(o_{j1},\dots,o_{jm})$, where
$o_{jk}$ requires a specific machine $\nu_{jk}$ and an uncertain processing
time known to lie in the interval
$\mathbf{p}_{jk}=[p^{L}_{jk},\,p^{U}_{jk}]$, $0\le p^L_{jk}\le p^U_{jk}$.
Each machine processes at most one operation at a time and preemption is
not allowed.
\end{definition}

A solution is a processing order of all $nm$ operations compatible with
job precedences; we encode it as a \emph{permutation with repetition} of
job indices, where the $k$-th occurrence of $j$ denotes $o_{jk}$. Given an
order, its \emph{semiactive schedule} starts every operation as early as
its job and machine predecessors permit. Under interval arithmetic, with
component-wise addition $[a,b]+[c,d]=[a+c,\,b+d]$ and join
$\max([a,b],[c,d])=[\max(a,c),\max(b,d)]$, starts and completions are
intervals:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_{o}=\max\!\big(\mathbf{c}_{JP(o)},\,\mathbf{c}_{MP(o)}\big),
\qquad
\mathbf{c}_{o}=\mathbf{s}_{o}+\mathbf{p}_{o},
\label{eq:semiactive}
\end{equation}
where $JP(o)$ and $MP(o)$ are the job and machine predecessors of $o$. The
interval makespan $\Cmax=[C^L_{\max},C^U_{\max}]$ is the maximum of job
completions, taken lexicographically by upper bound and, on ties, lower
bound---the ranking used throughout search, corresponding to min--max
robust optimization. For reporting we follow the field convention
\cite{DiazICAE2023,DiazTS2026}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RE}(\%)=\frac{\ECmax-\mathrm{LB}}{\mathrm{LB}}\times100,
\qquad \ECmax=\tfrac12\big(C^L_{\max}+C^U_{\max}\big),
\label{eq:re}
\end{equation}
with LB the published lower bound of the crisp Taillard
counterpart~\cite{Taillard1993}---an indicative, hardware-independent
yardstick rather than a strict optimality gap, since the crisp JSP and the
IJSP are different problems.

\begin{remark}
For instances whose intervals are symmetric perturbations of a crisp
instance, the midpoint scenario coincides with the original crisp
durations; comparisons under Eq.~\eqref{eq:re} are therefore
scenario-consistent (midpoint solution quality against the midpoint-scenario
bound).
\end{remark}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Dispatching as a Markov decision process}
\label{sec:mdp}

We formulate schedule construction as a finite-horizon MDP of exactly
$nm$ steps. The \emph{state} after $t$ steps comprises the partial
schedule (job pointers, job and machine completion intervals) and the set
$\mathcal{E}_t$ of eligible operations---the next unscheduled operation of
each unfinished job, so $1\le|\mathcal{E}_t|\le n$. The \emph{action}
selects one element of $\mathcal{E}_t$; the transition applies
Eq.~\eqref{eq:semiactive}. Episodes always terminate after $nm$ steps.

The \emph{reward} is a weighted sum of six shaping components, each
collapsing intervals by their worst case (upper bound): a terminal
makespan term (weight $1.0$; negative scaled makespan plus a bonus within
$5\%$ of the instance bound), a dense local-improvement term (weight
$0.3$; the step change of the projected makespan, penalizing
deteriorations twice as much as it rewards improvements), machine idleness
(weight $0.15$), machine load balance (weight $0.15$), operation
criticality (weight $0.05$) and job progress (weight $0.05$). The weights
were tuned through the benchmark-gated protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:protocol}; component scales adapt automatically to each
instance through its lower bound.

\subsection{Dimensionless, uncertainty-aware features}
\label{sec:features}

Let $\mathrm{LB}$ denote the instance lower bound (worst case if the bound
is itself an interval), used as the \emph{única} temporal scale. Each
eligible operation $o=o_{jk}$ on machine $\mu=\nu_{jk}$ is described by

context is the 12-dimensional aggregate of Table~\ref{tab:globalfeatures}.
Two design rules follow from ablation evidence
(Section~\ref{sec:ablations-neg}): (i) features must add information that
is \emph{not} a linear combination of others (interval widths enter as
relative, hence non-linear, magnitudes); and (ii) normalization must be
co-designed with initialization and learning rates rather than retrofitted.

\subsection{Size-invariant architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

Let $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$ be the features of eligible operation $i$ and
$g_0\in\mathbb{R}^{12}$ the global features. A shared two-layer MLP
$\phi$ (width $h{=}128$, LayerNorm, ReLU, orthogonal initialization)
embeds each candidate, $\phi_i=\phi(x_i)$. The context vector aggregates
the candidate set permutation-invariantly~\cite{Zaheer2017}:
\begin{equation}
g=\mathrm{MLP}_{ctx}\big(\big[\textstyle\frac1{|\mathcal{E}|}\sum_i\phi_i;\;
\max_i\phi_i;\;g_0\big]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{h}.
\end{equation}
The \emph{policy head} scores each candidate and applies a masked softmax,
and the \emph{value head} reads the context alone:
\begin{equation}
\pi(i\,|\,s)=\mathrm{softmax}_i\,\mathrm{MLP}_{\pi}([\phi_i;g]),
\qquad
V(s)=\mathrm{MLP}_{V}(g).
\end{equation}
No parameter dimension depends on $n$ or $m$: the same
${\approx}1.2\times10^{5}$-parameter network dispatches any instance. The
last policy layer is initialized with gain $10^{-2}$ so initial logits are
near-uniform. Optionally, a stack of $L$ pre-LN self-attention blocks
(multi-head, $4$ heads, no positional encoding, padding-masked) transforms
$\{\phi_i\}$ before pooling; $L{=}0$ recovers the base model exactly, and
Section~\ref{sec:ablations-attn} evaluates $L{=}2$.

During batched training, variable-size candidate sets are padded to the
batch maximum with a boolean mask applied to attention, pooling and
logits; we verify (unit tests) that masked-padded forward passes are
numerically identical to unpadded ones.

\subsection{Training and inference}
\label{sec:training}

We train with PPO~\cite{Schulman2017}; Table~\ref{tab:hyper} lists all
hyperparameters. Two choices depart from common JSP-DRL practice and are
enabled by co-design with the normalized representation: $\gamma=1$
(undiscounted finite horizon---with $\gamma=0.99$ the terminal makespan
signal reaches early decisions attenuated by $0.99^{nm}\!\approx\!0.05$
for $20{\times}15$ instances) and batched minibatch updates with
advantage normalization. Training on a set of instances proceeds in
blocks (one instance at a time) with full weight transfer; sampled
training episodes are interleaved with periodic greedy evaluations, and
the best solution/model is tracked across both.

\paragraph{Best-of-$N$ inference.} At evaluation time we exploit the
stochastic policy: one greedy rollout plus $N-1$ samples, keeping the best
solution by worst-case makespan ($N{=}64$). This choice is motivated
empirically in Section~\ref{sec:ablations-bon}: during training, the best
solutions frequently originate from sampled episodes, i.e.\ the policy is
more valuable as a \emph{distribution} than as its argmax---consistent
with sampling-based decoding in neural combinatorial
optimization~\cite{Kool2019}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Best-of-$N$ inference}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $(\sigma^{*},\Cmax^{*})\gets$ greedy rollout of $\pi$
\For{$i=1$ \textbf{to} $N-1$}
  \State $(\sigma,\Cmax)\gets$ sampled rollout of $\pi$
  \If{$\Cmax \prec_{lex} \Cmax^{*}$}
     $(\sigma^{*},\Cmax^{*})\gets(\sigma,\Cmax)$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\sigma^{*}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Instances, data split and metric}

We use the interval Taillard benchmark TA1--TA70 (seven size classes,
symmetric interval perturbations of the classic
instances~\cite{Taillard1993}; see~\cite{DiazICAE2023,DiazTS2026}).
The final model is trained on \textbf{TA11--TA14} only ($20{\times}15$);
\textbf{TA15--TA20} constituted the \emph{development set}: no gradients,
but every configuration decision was selected on their performance, so we
report them separately and exclude them from claims about unseen data.
All other instances are entirely unseen by both weights and development.
Three independent training seeds are reported as mean and best.
Performance is measured by RE, Eq.~\eqref{eq:re}, using the published
crisp lower bounds.

\subsection{Pre-registered, anchor-verified protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

All development followed a fixed protocol. (i) \emph{Two-stage
acceptance}: any proposed change was first evaluated on a cheap filter
benchmark (1 training instance, 30 episodes, 2 seeds) that can only
\emph{reject}; survivors ran the full benchmark (4 training instances,
100--1000 episodes, 3 seeds), whose pre-registered rule (mean improvement
beyond a noise threshold, or strict per-instance dominance) alone could
\emph{accept}. (ii) \emph{Paired seeds}: identical seeds across
configurations enable paired comparisons. (iii) \emph{Deterministic
anchors}: SPT, LPT, MOR and MWKR results are recomputed in every run and
verified identical across all code versions---any deviation indicates an
inadvertent change of environment semantics and invalidates the
comparison. (iv) \emph{Bit-reproducibility}: rerunning a configuration
with the same seed reproduces results exactly. Every accepted and
rejected experiment, with its data, is versioned in the project
repository. This methodology caught, among others, thirteen
superficially plausible single-factor modifications whose filter or
confirmation results were clearly negative.

\subsection{Hyperparameter configuration}
\label{sec:hyperconfig}

All reported results use the literature-standard hyperparameters of
Table~\ref{tab:hyper} \emph{without problem-specific tuning}. To assess
whether this leaves performance on the table, we ran an automatic
configuration study with irace~\cite{LopezIbanez2016}---the same tool and
methodology used to configure the tabu search
of~\cite{DiazTS2026}---racing an 8-parameter space (learning rate, entropy
coefficient, PPO clip, epochs, minibatch size, update period, GAE
$\lambda$, hidden width) over a budget of $330$ tuning experiments at
reduced fidelity ($1$ training instance, $300$ episodes, ${\sim}8$ minutes
per evaluation), with training seeds as racing instances. The five elite
configurations agreed on a coherent faster-learning regime (learning rate
$7$--$9{\cdot}10^{-4}$, clip $0.3$, minibatch $128$, updates every $2$
episodes, $\lambda{=}0.90$) and were insensitive to network width.
However, at the full $1000$-episode operating budget the best elite scored
$14.5\%$ mean RE versus $13.4\%$ for the defaults ($+1.66\%$ mean
makespan; five of six development instances within noise, one
significantly worse) at ${\sim}60\%$ higher training cost: the
low-fidelity optimum did not transfer.

To rule out that this non-transfer was merely an artifact of the reduced
tuning fidelity, we ran a second, \emph{operating-fidelity} campaign:
$300$ experiments each training at the full $1000$ episodes on two
instances (enabled by a ${\sim}3\times$ GPU-vectorized rollout
implementation), over a $6$-parameter space seeded with the defaults and
the low-fidelity elites. This campaign converged to a coherent region
\emph{distinct} from the defaults (clip $0.1$, $8$ epochs, $\lambda{=}0.90$,
width $256$, learning rate $5$--$9{\cdot}10^{-4}$) and eliminated the
seeded default during racing. Yet under the same pre-registered
confirmation as every other design decision---its winning configuration
retrained on the full four-instance set over three seeds and evaluated
against the default checkpoints with an identical protocol---it again
failed to improve on the default: $14.73\%$ versus $13.52\%$ mean RE over
the six development instances ($+1.21$ points; better on two instances,
worse on four). Two conclusions follow, now on firm footing: automatic
configuration does not beat the literature defaults for this problem
\emph{even when performed at the operating budget}, and the defaults sit
on the achievable performance plateau---our results do not depend on fine
tuning. The recurring gap between racing-optimal and confirmation-optimal
configurations is itself a cautionary observation for automatic DRL
configuration, where single-run training cost is too noisy to separate
near-equivalent configurations.

\subsection{Baselines}

Constructive baselines comprise priority dispatching rules (SPT, LPT, MOR,
MWKR, with interval-aware lexicographic comparisons; MOR is uniformly the
strongest rule) and the Giffler--Thompson active schedule
generator~\cite{GifflerThompson1960} with rule tie-breaks inside the
machine conflict set. G\&T with MWKR tie-break is the strongest
deterministic constructive baseline on this benchmark: $29.4\%$ mean RE
across the 70 instances, versus $45.4\%$ for plain MOR dispatching (the
SPT tie-break variant performs poorly, $70.6\%$). Published IJSP
metaheuristics (fEABC~\cite{DiazFEABC2023}, ESABC~\cite{DiazICAE2023},
TS~\cite{DiazTS2026}; 30 runs and minutes of per-instance search on
dedicated hardware) are included as reference yardsticks from a different
computational class rather than as direct competitors.

As a \emph{learned symbolic} baseline we evolve dispatching rules by
genetic programming~\cite{Branke2016}: expression trees over
interval-aware operation attributes (worst-case processing time, earliest
start, work remaining, remaining operations, slack, and the interval
widths of the first three), with protected arithmetic, $\min$/$\max$ and
negation as functions. The GP uses the \emph{same} four training
instances as the policy (TA11--TA14) and mean RE of the deterministic
rollout as fitness; population $100$, $50$ generations, tournament
selection of size $4$, subtree crossover ($0.8$) and mutation, elitism
$2$, tree size capped at $40$ nodes, and SPT and MWKR injected as seed
individuals. Following the baseline-symmetry principle of
Section~\ref{sec:hyperconfig}, its parameters are conventional literature
settings without tuning; three independent seeds converged to
$15.60$--$15.87\%$ training RE, so no tuning safeguard was triggered. For calibration
across the full benchmark: best-of-$1024$ policy sampling attains $12.7\%$
mean RE over the 70 instances---ahead of every deterministic constructive
baseline on every instance, within $2.2$--$4.6$ points of the fEABC
30-run average per class, and behind tuned tabu search everywhere.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{In-size performance and budget scaling}
\label{sec:results-insize}

Table~\ref{tab:insize} reports per-instance RE on the development set for
increasing training budgets, and Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} the aggregate
picture. Three observations: (i) the policy scales monotonically with
budget ($28.0\%\to16.4\%\to13.4\%$ mean RE at $100/300/1000$ episodes per
instance) with clearly diminishing returns at the last step, locating the

\subsection{Ablation: best-of-$N$ inference}
\label{sec:ablations-bon}

Introducing best-of-64 inference (over one greedy rollout) improved the
full-benchmark mean makespan by $3.6\%$, strictly better on 3 of 6
development instances and worse on none, while sharply reducing seed
variance. Diagnostically, before this change the best training solutions
frequently arose in early, exploratory episodes and were never surpassed
by the greedy policy---evidence that the policy's value concentrates in
its sampling distribution.

\subsection{Ablation: attention versus pooling}
\label{sec:ablations-attn}

Two pre-LN self-attention blocks over the candidate embeddings (identical
code path, one flag) were evaluated at both operating points with paired
seeds: $+1.2\%$ mean makespan at $300$ episodes (all six instances within
the noise threshold) and $+1.6\%$ at $1000$ (one instance significantly
worse), at ${\approx}30\%$ higher per-episode cost
(Table~\ref{tab:insize-attn}). We conclude that at these budgets the
pooled context already conveys the interaction information the policy can
exploit; richer pairwise structure may require larger budgets or
instance distributions to pay off. This negative result is reported with
the same rigor as the positive ones.

\subsection{Ablation: specialist versus multi-size training}
\label{sec:ablations-neg}

Size invariance enables training on mixed size classes, a natural
hypothesis for improving transfer. We trained agents on twelve instances
spanning $15{\times}15$, $20{\times}15$ and $20{\times}20$, at matched
total budget ($3000$ episodes) and at matched \emph{per-instance} budget
($12{,}000$ episodes), against the $20{\times}15$ specialist. The
specialist won on \emph{all} five common evaluation instances in both
settings---including instances of the size classes the multi-size agent
itself trained on (budget-equalized: $8.7$ vs.\ $9.5$ on $15{\times}15$;
$15.9$ vs.\ $18.5$ on $20{\times}20$; $24.5$ vs.\ $28.3$ on the unseen
$30{\times}20$). Per-instance training depth dominates size diversity,
and---contrary to the intuition that transfer requires diverse
training---specialist generalization is the stronger phenomenon. Earlier
development iterations provide the complementary caution: appending
features that are linear combinations of existing ones, or retrofitting
input normalization onto a system co-adapted to raw scales, degraded
performance by $9$--$113\%$; representation changes must be co-designed
with optimization.

\subsection{Learned symbolic baseline: GP-evolved rules}
\label{sec:results-gp}

Table~\ref{tab:gp} reports the generalization of the three GP-evolved
rules to all 70 Taillard instances (a single deterministic rollout per
instance; the rules were evolved on $20{\times}15$ only). The best rule
attains $18.5\%$ mean RE---beating G\&T-MWKR ($29.4\%$) by ${\approx}11$
points and every fixed dispatching rule by a wide margin---and transfers
across sizes without retraining; its best class is in fact the unseen
$50{\times}15$. Structurally, the evolved expressions are recognizable as
MWKR corrected by earliest-start and slack$\times$processing-time terms.

Because training budgets are already comparable (GP: $20{,}400$ fitness
rollouts in ${\approx}49$ min/seed; policy: $4{,}000$ training episodes
in ${\approx}80$ min/seed; identical training instances), the remaining
axis to equalize is \emph{inference} budget. Table~\ref{tab:fairmatrix}
compares both learners single-shot and at matched best-of-$1024$; for
the GP rule, sampling uses GRASP-style $\epsilon{=}0.1$ randomization
(sample $0$ deterministic, mirroring the policy protocol). Two findings.
First, \emph{single-shot, the interpretable rule matches the neural
policy}: $18.5\%$ versus $19.4\%$ for the greedy policy---the policy's
point estimate is not where its value lies. Second, under an equal
sampling budget the ranking reverses and the gap reopens: the policy
converts $1024$ samples into a $6.7$-point improvement, uniform noise
around the best evolvable formula only $4.4$ points. The net
contribution of deep RL in this domain is therefore the \emph{quality of
the learned sampling distribution}, not a stronger greedy heuristic---
consistent with the best-of-$N$ ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:ablations-bon}. (The policy pool mixes three training
seeds while GP-$\epsilon$ uses the single best rule; the three evolved
rules are near-identical, $15.60$--$15.87\%$ training RE, so the effect
of this asymmetry is minor.)

\subsection{Positioning and computational cost}

Training the final model takes ${\approx}80$ minutes per seed
($4{\times}1000$ episodes) on a commodity CPU; constructing a solution
takes milliseconds (greedy) to a few seconds (best-of-64 on
$50{\times}20$). The policy's standalone RE ($13.4\%$ in-size;
$7$--$25\%$ zero-shot) sits between dispatching rules ($46\%$) and
per-instance metaheuristics ($4$--$10\%$)---establishing, for the IJSP,
the same computational-class trade-off documented for the deterministic
JSP~\cite{Zhang2020L2D}, and motivating the hybrid use studied next.

\section{Toward Hybridization: Seeding Tabu Search Populations}
\label{sec:hybrid}

The tabu search of~\cite{DiazTS2026} initializes each run with a
population of $250$ individuals. We propose seeding a fraction $p$ of the
population with policy samples, and characterize first the raw material:
for three pilot instances---clean for both methods (outside RL
training/development and outside the TS irace tuning set)---we sampled
$1024$ solutions from the policy and from two randomized dispatching
generators (MOR with $\epsilon$-greedy noise, $\epsilon{=}0.1$; and a
mixed-rule GRASP-style generator~\cite{Feo1995}).
Figure~\ref{fig:pools} shows the RE distributions: the \emph{median}
policy sample is better than the \emph{best} of $1024$ heuristic
individuals on all three instances, by $15$--$25$ RE points
(e.g.\ TA22: policy best/median $13.2\%/22.9\%$; MOR-$\epsilon$
best $43.8\%$).

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We presented the first learned constructive policy for the interval job
shop scheduling problem, built on a dimensionless, uncertainty-aware
state representation that makes policy and value networks independent of
the instance size. A single network trained on four instances of one size
class dominates the best priority dispatching rule on the entire Taillard
IJSP grid in zero-shot evaluation, and scales predictably with training
budget on its own class. Our pre-registered, anchor-verified methodology
additionally establishes two negative results we believe are valuable to
the community: at practical budgets, self-attention over candidates does
not improve on pooled context, and multi-size training does not improve
on---indeed loses to---a single-size specialist, even budget-equalized.

The policy's sampling distribution makes it a strong initializer for
population-based search, dominating randomized dispatching generators by
wide margins; the ongoing hybridization study quantifies the resulting
anytime gains within a state-of-the-art tabu search. Beyond it, we see
three directions: instance distributions with heterogeneous uncertainty,
where min--max and expected-value criteria genuinely diverge; curricula
and fine-tuning protocols that exploit size invariance; and tighter
integrations where the policy guides neighbourhood construction rather
than only initialization.
