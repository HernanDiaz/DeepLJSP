\title{\textbf{A Size-Invariant Deep Reinforcement Learning Policy\\
for the Interval Job Shop Scheduling Problem}}

\author{Hern\'an D\'iaz \and \todo{co-authors}}
\date{Draft --- July 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
We present a constructive deep reinforcement learning (DRL) policy for the
Interval Job Shop Scheduling Problem (IJSP), in which task processing times
are only known to lie within closed intervals. The policy scores eligible
operations through a Deep Sets encoder over dimensionless, per-operation
uncertainty-aware features combined with a pooled global context, making both
the policy and value networks \emph{independent of the instance size}: a
single trained network dispatches instances of any number of jobs and
machines. Trained with PPO on only four $20{\times}15$ Taillard IJSP
instances, the policy reaches a relative error of $13.4\%$ (mean of three
seeds; $12.3\%$ best) with respect to the crisp Taillard lower bounds on
unseen $20{\times}15$ instances, clearly outperforming the best priority
dispatching rule (MOR, ${\approx}46\%$) while producing solutions in
milliseconds. Zero-shot, the same network beats MOR on every tested instance
of all seven Taillard size classes, from $15{\times}15$ to $50{\times}20$.
Controlled ablations show that (i) sampling-based best-of-$N$ inference
provides a consistent improvement, (ii) self-attention over the eligible
operations does not improve over pooling at practical training budgets, and
(iii) a specialist trained on a single size class generalizes better across
sizes than a multi-size-trained counterpart, even at an equalized
per-instance budget. Finally, we characterize the quality of the policy as a
population initializer for a state-of-the-art tabu search, laying out a
pre-registered hybridization study.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Job Shop Scheduling Problem (JSP) is a classical NP-hard combinatorial
optimization problem. In many practical settings, task durations are not
known precisely at scheduling time; the \emph{interval} JSP (IJSP) models
this uncertainty by specifying each processing time as a closed interval,
and asks for a robust operation ordering evaluated under interval
arithmetic~\cite{DiazICAE2023,DiazFEABC2023}. State-of-the-art IJSP solvers
are metaheuristics---artificial bee colony variants~\cite{DiazFEABC2023,
DiazICAE2023} and, more recently, tabu search with specialized
neighbourhoods~\cite{DiazTS2026}---which optimize each instance individually
under a per-instance search budget of minutes.

An orthogonal line of research learns \emph{constructive} policies for
scheduling with deep reinforcement learning (DRL)~\cite{Zhang2020L2D,
Kool2019}. Such policies build a schedule in a single dispatching pass
(milliseconds to seconds) and, once trained, amortize their cost across
instances. To our knowledge, no learned constructive policy had been
evaluated on the IJSP.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{size-invariant DRL policy for the IJSP}: per-operation
  features are dimensionless (normalized by an instance lower bound) and
  uncertainty-aware; a Deep Sets~\cite{Zaheer2017} encoder with pooled
  global context yields policy and value networks whose parameters are
  independent of the numbers of jobs and machines.
  \item An \textbf{empirical scaling and generalization study} on the
  Taillard IJSP benchmark: the policy improves monotonically with training
  budget on its training size class and transfers zero-shot to all seven
  size classes, dominating the best dispatching rule everywhere.
  \item \textbf{Controlled ablations} with paired seeds and deterministic
  anchors: best-of-$N$ inference, self-attention versus pooling, and
  specialist versus multi-size training.
  \item A \textbf{pre-registered hybridization design} that uses the policy
  to seed the initial population of a state-of-the-art tabu search,
  including a characterization of seed-pool quality against randomized
  dispatching baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Interval JSP.} The IJSP evaluates schedules with interval
arithmetic and ranks interval makespans either by their expected value
(midpoint) or lexicographically by upper bound~\cite{DiazICAE2023}.
Population-based metaheuristics constitute the state of the art:
\emph{fEABC}~\cite{DiazFEABC2023} and \emph{ESABC}~\cite{DiazICAE2023}
report mean relative errors of roughly $5$--$14\%$ on the Taillard IJSP set,
and irace-tuned tabu search reduces this to $0$--$12\%$ depending on the
size class~\cite{DiazTS2026}.

\paragraph{DRL for scheduling.} Learning-to-dispatch approaches encode the
scheduling state and score eligible operations with neural networks trained
by policy-gradient methods~\cite{Zhang2020L2D}; attention-based encoders
are standard in neural combinatorial optimization~\cite{Kool2019}. Sampling
multiple rollouts at inference (best-of-$N$) is a common, effective
decoder~\cite{Kool2019}. Our work differs in the uncertainty setting
(intervals), in the explicitly dimensionless feature design that yields
size invariance for both heads, and in the systematic ablation methodology.

\section{The Interval Job Shop Scheduling Problem}

An IJSP instance consists of $n$ jobs and $m$ machines; each job is a fixed
sequence of $m$ operations, one per machine, and each operation $o$ has an
interval duration $\mathbf{p}_o=[p^L_o,\,p^U_o]$. A solution is an operation
processing order compatible with job precedences; its \emph{semiactive}
schedule assigns to each operation the earliest start compatible with its
job and machine predecessors, computed with interval arithmetic:
$\mathbf{s}_o=\max(\mathbf{c}_{jp(o)},\,\mathbf{c}_{mp(o)})$ with the
component-wise maximum, and $\mathbf{c}_o=\mathbf{s}_o+\mathbf{p}_o$. The
interval makespan $\mathbf{C}_{\max}$ is the lexicographic maximum (by upper
bound, then lower) of job completions. Following~\cite{DiazICAE2023},
solutions are ranked during optimization by the worst case $C^U_{\max}$
(with the lower bound as tie-breaker), and reported using the expected
makespan $\ECmax$ (interval midpoint) through the relative error
\[
\mathrm{RE}(\%) \;=\; \frac{\ECmax - \mathrm{LB}}{\mathrm{LB}} \times 100,
\]
where LB is the published lower bound of the crisp Taillard
counterpart~\cite{Taillard1993}. As in prior IJSP work, these crisp bounds
are an indicative, hardware-independent yardstick rather than a strict
optimality gap.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Dispatching as a Markov decision process}

A schedule is built in $nm$ steps. At each step the agent observes the set
of \emph{eligible} operations (the next unscheduled operation of each
unfinished job) and selects one; the environment appends it to the schedule
and updates job and machine completion intervals. The reward combines a
terminal makespan term with dense shaping components (idle time, load
balance, local bound improvement) whose weights were tuned by a
benchmark-gated search; all reward components collapse intervals by their
worst case.

\subsection{Size-invariant state representation}

Each eligible operation is described by a $16$-dimensional vector of
\emph{dimensionless} quantities: duration, earliest start and remaining job
work (lower and upper bounds divided by the instance lower bound
$\mathrm{LB}$), their relative interval widths, job progress, remaining
operation count fraction, machine context (remaining machine load
$/\mathrm{LB}$, potential idle gap $/\mathrm{LB}$, relative machine
congestion), slack against the earliest eligible operation, and normalized
machine/job completion times. A $12$-dimensional global context aggregates
the schedule state exclusively through means, maxima and standard
deviations of per-machine and per-job quantities, plus the pending relative
uncertainty. No feature depends on $n$ or $m$ through its dimension.

\subsection{Architecture}

A shared MLP $\phi$ (two layers, width $128$, LayerNorm) embeds each
eligible operation. The global embedding $g$ is an MLP over the
concatenation of masked mean- and max-pooling of $\{\phi_i\}$ with the
global features (a Deep Sets construction~\cite{Zaheer2017}). The policy
head scores each candidate as $\mathrm{MLP}([\phi_i;g])$ followed by a
masked softmax; the value head is an MLP over $g$ alone. Both heads are
therefore size-invariant. An optional stack of pre-LN self-attention blocks
over $\{\phi_i\}$ (evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:ablations}) can be
inserted before pooling.

\subsection{Training and inference}

The agent is trained with PPO~\cite{Schulman2017} using minibatch updates
(256 transitions, 4 epochs), GAE ($\lambda{=}0.95$), advantage
normalization, gradient clipping, orthogonal initialization and
$\gamma{=}1$ (finite horizon, terminal-dominated reward). Training
alternates sampled episodes with periodic greedy evaluations used for model
selection. At test time we use \textbf{best-of-$N$} inference: one greedy
rollout plus $N{-}1$ policy samples, returning the best solution by worst
case ($N{=}64$ throughout).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Instances and data split.} We use the Taillard IJSP benchmark
(TA1--TA70, seven size classes)~\cite{DiazICAE2023,Taillard1993}. The final
model is trained on \textbf{TA11--TA14} only ($20{\times}15$);
\textbf{TA15--TA20} served as the development set for all configuration
decisions and are reported separately; all remaining instances are entirely
unseen. Three independent training seeds are reported as mean and best.

\paragraph{Protocol.} All comparisons run under a fixed benchmark harness
with per-seed bit-reproducibility. Deterministic dispatching rules (SPT,
LPT, MOR, MWKR) act as \emph{anchors}: their results are verified to be
identical across all code versions, guaranteeing that improvements are
attributable to learning and not to environment changes. Configuration
changes were accepted only under a pre-registered two-stage rule (a cheap
filter benchmark, then a full confirmation benchmark).

\paragraph{Baselines.} Priority dispatching rules (best: MOR); published
IJSP solvers fEABC~\cite{DiazFEABC2023}, ESABC~\cite{DiazICAE2023} and tabu
search~\cite{DiazTS2026} as reference yardsticks (30 runs, minutes of
search per instance, different computational class).

\section{Results}

\subsection{In-size performance and scaling}

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} and Table~\ref{tab:scaling} show the development
of mean RE on TA15--TA20 as the per-instance training budget grows. The
policy scales monotonically ($28.0\%$ at $100$ episodes, $16.4\%$ at $300$,
$13.4\%$ at $1000$) with clearly diminishing returns at the last step,
locating the pure-budget ceiling around $11$--$12\%$. Seed variance
collapses with budget (e.g.\ per-instance standard deviations of
$13$--$24$ makespan units at $300$ episodes). Best-of-$64$ inference
contributes a consistent improvement over a single greedy rollout
($-3.6\%$ mean makespan, better on 3/6 development instances with none
worse, at its introduction point).

\subsection{Zero-shot cross-size generalization}

Because no network dimension depends on the instance size, the
$20{\times}15$ specialist evaluates directly on any size class.
Figure~\ref{fig:crosssize} reports RE on unseen instances of all seven
classes: the policy beats MOR on \textbf{every} tested instance, with RE
ranging from $7.0\%$ ($15{\times}15$) to $24.5$--$25.0\%$ on the hardest
transfer classes ($30{\times}20$, $50{\times}15$), and no degradation cliff
up to $1000$-operation episodes ($50{\times}20$: $15.1\%$ versus MOR's
$48.7\%$).

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

\paragraph{Attention versus pooling.} Adding two self-attention blocks over
the eligible operations---identical code path, one flag---yields parity at
$300$ episodes ($+1.2\%$, all six development instances within noise) and
parity-to-worse at $1000$ ($+1.6\%$, one instance significantly worse), at
${\sim}30\%$ higher per-episode cost (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). At these
budgets, pooled context already captures the necessary interaction
information.

\paragraph{Specialist versus multi-size training.} Training one agent on
twelve instances of three size classes ($15{\times}15$, $20{\times}15$,
$20{\times}20$) loses to the single-size specialist on \emph{all} five
common evaluation instances---including the size classes the multi-size
agent itself trained on---both at matched total budget ($3000$ episodes)
and at matched \emph{per-instance} budget ($12{,}000$ episodes; e.g.\
$9.5$ vs.\ $8.7$ on TA5, $18.5$ vs.\ $15.9$ on TA25-class, $28.3$ vs.\
$24.5$ on TA41-class). Per-instance depth dominates size diversity, and
specialist generalization is the stronger phenomenon.

\subsection{Positioning against search-based solvers}

The learned policy occupies a distinct computational class: it constructs a
solution in milliseconds (best-of-64: seconds) versus minutes of
per-instance search for the metaheuristics. Its final in-size RE
($13.4\%$) remains above fEABC ($9.6\%$) and far from tuned tabu search
($3.9\%$); its value lies in amortized speed, in full cross-size
generality from a single network, and---as explored next---as an
initializer for search.

\section{Toward Hybridization: Seeding Tabu Search Populations}
\label{sec:hybrid}

The tabu search of~\cite{DiazTS2026} starts each run from a population of
$250$ individuals. We propose seeding a fraction $p$ of this population
with policy samples. Figure~\ref{fig:pools} characterizes seed-pool quality
on three clean pilot instances (excluded from RL training/development and
from the TS irace tuning): the \emph{median} policy sample is better than
the \emph{best} individual produced by randomized dispatching baselines
(MOR with $\epsilon$-greedy noise, and a mixed-rule GRASP-style generator)
by $15$--$25$ RE points.

The pre-registered pilot compares five arms (control; $p\in\{10,50,100\%\}$
with policy seeds; $p{=}50\%$ with MOR-$\epsilon$ seeds) under paired RNG
seeds, recording anytime curves. Hypotheses: (H1) policy seeding reduces
time-to-target; (H2) the benefit exceeds that of heuristic seeding at equal
$p$; (H3) an intermediate $p$ is optimal due to diversity loss at
$p{=}100\%$. \todo{pilot results pending}

\section{Conclusions}

We introduced a size-invariant constructive DRL policy for the IJSP that
dominates priority dispatching rules across the entire Taillard grid from a
single network trained on four instances of one size class, and
characterized its scaling, its inference-time sampling gains, and two
negative results of methodological value (attention parity; specialist
over multi-size training). The policy's sample distributions position it as
a strong population initializer for state-of-the-art search, which the
ongoing hybridization study quantifies. Future work includes training-time
curricula exploiting the size invariance, uncertainty-heterogeneous
instances where ranking criteria diverge, and end-to-end integration with
neighbourhood search.
