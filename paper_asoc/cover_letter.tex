I submit for your consideration the manuscript \emph{Genetic
Programming Hyper-Heuristics for the Job Shop Scheduling Problem with
Interval Durations: Robustness-Aware Interpretable Rules that Generalize
Across Instance Sizes}, as a technical paper.

In many manufacturing settings the duration of an operation is not
known in advance, and a planner can only give a lower and an upper
bound. The interval job shop scheduling problem models this imprecision
without assuming any probability distribution, and its best solutions
so far come from metaheuristics that search anew for every instance.
The manuscript brings genetic programming to this problem. A
hyper-heuristic evolves dispatching rules that read both the worst-case
value and the width of each uncertain quantity. The rules build a
schedule in milliseconds and are short enough to be read and checked by
a planner.

The contribution has three parts. First, rules evolved on four
instances improve on every constructive baseline on all seventy
instances of the benchmark, and transfer without retraining to all size
classes and to a second benchmark. Second, ablations show where interval
information pays off: not in the expected makespan, but in the
predictability of the schedule. An objective that penalizes the width of
the predicted makespan yields schedules whose execution departs less
from the prediction, with a single weight that sets the trade-off.
Third, a comparison at matched budget with a genetic algorithm,
reimplemented on the same decoder, shows up to which computational
budget the evolved rule is the better choice, and how that budget grows
with instance size.

The work fits the scope of the journal on two counts. It applies
evolutionary computing to a real-world complexity, uncertain processing
times. And it does so in the spirit the journal describes: it exploits
the tolerance for imprecision that intervals express to obtain
schedules at low solution cost, and examines what robustness that
tolerance can buy. The manuscript also builds on genetic programming
hyper-heuristics for scheduling published in the journal.

I confirm that the manuscript is original, that it has not been
published elsewhere, and that it is not under consideration by another
journal. An earlier version of this study is available as a preprint
(SSRN, doi:10.2139/ssrn.7214286).

The complete source code is openly available in Zenodo
(doi:10.5281/zenodo.21716972; version 2.0, which accompanies this
manuscript: doi:10.5281/zenodo.23156759): the hyper-heuristic, the
baselines and the genetic algorithm, together with a test that
re-derives the deposited results from scratch. The deposit also holds
the instances, the evolved rules and the result files behind every
table and figure. A statement on the use of generative AI in the
preparation of the manuscript appears directly before the references,
as the journal's policy requires.

Thank you for considering this work.
